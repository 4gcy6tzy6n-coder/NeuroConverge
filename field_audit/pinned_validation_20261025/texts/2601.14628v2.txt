ABSTRACT
Vision-language-action (VLA) models inherit powerful semantic representations from large language models, but carrying over
their design priorities can leave the demands of physical control under-specified. Stable manipulation requires high-frequency
proprioceptive feedback and persistent motor state, in addition to instruction understanding. Here we introduce Neuromorphic
Vision-Language-Action (NeuroVLA), a brain-inspired architecture that couples a cortical vision-language planner to recurrent
cerebellar modulation and a stateful spiking spinal controller. These components translate semantic intent into actions
while incorporating high-frequency proprioceptive and force feedback, maintaining short-term motor memory, and enabling
local responses to unexpected contact. In simulated and physical manipulation tasks, the cerebellar module reduces mean
commanded jerk by 75.6%, and NeuroVLA achieves a 54.8% collision-recovery rate compared with 0.0% for the evaluated
VLA baselines. The spinal controller implemented on an FPGA-based neuromorphic processor achieves 2.19 ms inference
latency and 0.87 mJ per inference, corresponding to approximately 0.4 W during inference. These results motivate moving
beyond inherited language-model design priorities toward embodied architectures that explicitly integrate semantic planning,
high-frequency bodily feedback, motor memory, and local correction.

1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend pretrained language and vision-language models to robotic control, transferring
semantic knowledge and instruction-following capabilities1, 2 . Their action interfaces range from token prediction to specialized
continuous-action experts3–5 . We argue that this productive continuity can also create architectural inertia: design priorities
established for language modeling continue to shape embodied systems, even when physical interaction calls for different
feedback rates, internal states, and response pathways. Larger semantic models and more expressive action outputs do not, by
themselves, specify how a controller should sense rapid bodily changes, retain motor context, or react locally to contact.
Embodied intelligence therefore requires explicit attention to the design of the motor system. High-frequency proprioception
grounds control in the current physical state; persistent motor dynamics retain the recent history needed for coordinated
execution; and local feedback pathways support rapid correction between semantic updates. These are engineering counterparts
of biological sensorimotor principles, including the stateful, practiced execution often described as “muscle memory.” Their
relevance is concrete: pouring liquid requires continuous stabilization as the load changes, shaking a flask requires tracking the
phase and progress of repeated motions, and recovering from contact requires responding before a slow visual inference cycle
completes.
These tasks expose three related control bottlenecks. First, low-rate, vision-dominant feedback can miss rapid changes
in joint state or contact force, allowing oscillation and trajectory drift to persist. Second, action decoders without persistent
motor state must infer temporal progress from successive observations, making rhythmic and partially occluded tasks difficult.
Third, routing every fine-grained adjustment through a large foundation model couples response time and computational cost
to semantic inference. Efficient decoding and execution strategies address aspects of these problems6, 7, 49 ; a complementary
approach is to give local motor control access to bodily feedback and its own temporal state.
Biological motor systems motivate this separation of responsibilities. Cortical planning interacts with cerebellar prediction
and correction, while spinal circuits integrate descending commands with local feedback8, 11, 44 . We adopt these functional
principles without assuming a one-to-one correspondence between engineered modules and anatomical subdivisions. In

persistent motor state, and local correction as useful complements to high-level instruction understanding.

2 Results
2.1 A Brain-inspired VLA with Event-driven Sparsity
NeuroVLA separates semantic interpretation, feedback-based modulation, and action generation (Fig. 1). The cortical module
processes images and language instructions with a pretrained vision-language model. A Q-Former extracts compact, taskrelevant intention tokens from its hidden representations16 , providing the downstream controller with an action-oriented
semantic interface.
The cerebellar module integrates a history of joint states and force/torque measurements through recurrent dynamics. Gated
feature-wise linear modulation then adjusts the semantic latent according to the current physical context. This mechanism
makes local stabilization and contact-aware correction possible without requiring a new cortical inference for every adjustment.
Its functional inspiration is cerebellar prediction and feedback control11, 17 .
The spinal module converts the modulated latent into continuous actions using stateful spiking neurons. Membrane
dynamics carry information across control steps, while selective spiking activity provides an event-driven execution substrate.
In the implementation described in Methods, cortical and cerebellar computations run on the CUDA tier, and the spinal
controller is validated on an FPGA-based neuromorphic processor. The following experiments examine semantic selection,
feedback modulation, temporal integration, and physical performance in turn; architectural details are provided in Methods 4.

2.2 Task intent drives top-down attentional gating of spatial primitives
To examine how semantic intent selects spatial information, we visualized the Q-Former’s cross-modal attention under different
instructions in the same scene (Fig. 2). For “Put the wine bottle on top of the cabinet,” attention first concentrated on the bottle
and then shifted toward the placement surface as execution progressed. When the instruction changed to “Open the middle
drawer,” attention instead concentrated on the drawer handle despite the bottle remaining visible.
These observations show instruction-dependent spatial selection, consistent with the Q-Former’s role as an information
bottleneck between visual-language features and motor control. They support task-relevant filtering before feedback-based
motor modulation and action generation.
2.3 Proprioceptive feedback stabilizes motion and supports local correction
To investigate the mechanistic basis of the system’s robustness, we examined three control functions: stabilization during
free-space motion, local correction after contact, and temporal coordination during rhythmic manipulation. These functions
motivate the cerebellar design, but the experiments assess robot behaviour rather than identifying separate anatomical loops.
Proprioceptive stabilization of ongoing motion

We first tested whether feedback-based modulation reduces high-frequency fluctuations in commanded motion. In free-space
trajectories, the baseline policy produced pronounced acceleration oscillations, whereas activating the cerebellar module
attenuated these fluctuations (Fig. 4a).
Using end-effector pose, joint angles, and joint velocities as state feedback, the module reduced mean commanded jerk
by 75.6%, with reductions of 80.2% in ∆yaw and 80.0% in ∆Z (Fig. 4b). Mean Absolute Commanded Acceleration (MACA)
decreased by 32.8% to 58.0% across axes (Fig. 4c). These measurements demonstrate smoother command trajectories under
the evaluated conditions.
Force-aware correction after unexpected contact

We next tested whether local force feedback enables adaptation when an intended trajectory is obstructed. Contact generated
sharp perturbations in the 6-DoF wrench profile (Fig. 3b), including mean force values of µ ≈ −37.02 in Fx and µ ≈ 8.30 in
Fy , with variance σ 2 ≈ 3.27 in Fy .
The vision-centric baseline continued along the blocked path (red dashed line). NeuroVLA used the high-frequency
(> 200 Hz) force stream to adjust the local trajectory and navigate around the contact point (blue solid line). This result
supports force-aware motor correction through the feedback pathway. The force/torque sensor measures contact state; it is not
treated as a substitute for a biological vestibular organ.
Temporal coordination during rhythmic manipulation

In the “Shake the cup” task, three oscillatory cycles were visible in synchronized wrench and joint-angle traces (Fig. 3a). The
alignment of these trajectories indicates coordinated rhythmic execution, including periods when visual feedback was static or
occluded. These synchronized traces demonstrate coordinated rhythmic execution; the spinal temporal contribution is assessed
by the ablation below.
Under degraded visual observations, the cerebellar-enhanced agent also maintained higher success than the evaluated
cortical baselines (Fig. 5e). This supports the usefulness of proprioceptive context when visual information is unreliable. The

2.4 Event-driven sparsity and temporal integration in the neuromorphic spinal substrate
We next examined whether spinal activity follows actuation demand and whether persistent spiking state contributes to task
performance. During “Static Pose & Dynamic Gripper” phases, end-effector pose neurons remained relatively quiescent while
gripper-control neurons fired more actively (Fig. 5a,b). Mean activation rates were lower during static holding than during
dynamic actions (Fig. 5c). These observations demonstrate selective, demand-dependent neural activity; hardware energy
consumption is evaluated separately below.
Stateful spiking neurons carry temporal information by accumulating and decaying membrane voltage19 . On LIBERO23, 37 ,
the multi-step SNN achieved higher success than a single-step variant, particularly in tasks requiring transitions between
manipulation phases (Fig. 5d). The same panel includes a no-cerebellum baseline. The temporal ablation supports the
contribution of persistent spiking state, while the complementary baseline evaluates the contribution of cerebellar modulation.
Firing-rate maps conditioned on kinematic dimensions showed distinct activation patterns (Fig. 6, Neural Representation of
Action), and t-SNE projections separated several action-associated latent states (Action Latent Cluster). These visualizations
suggest functional specialization within the learned controller. They provide descriptive evidence of representation structure
rather than demonstrating a biological somatotopic map or establishing the cause of the performance gains.
2.5 Hardware validation of the spiking spinal controller
We implemented the spinal controller on an FPGA-based neuromorphic processor (Fig. 7a). Its systolic array updates multiple
leaky integrate-and-fire neurons29 in parallel, reusing spikes across columns and weights across rows (Fig. 7b). A spike-detection
module suppresses inactive events to reduce unnecessary updates41, 42 .
At an operating frequency of 20 MHz, the processor achieved 2.19 ms inference latency and 0.87 mJ per inference,
corresponding to approximately 0.4 W during inference. Resource utilization and performance are summarized in Fig. 7c,d.
These measurements characterize the spinal controller on the FPGA implementation; they do not include cortical and cerebellar
GPU computation, sensing, communication, or robot actuation.

2.6 Physical manipulation and collision recovery
We deployed NeuroVLA on a physical bimanual humanoid platform to test spatial precision, fluid handling, and rhythmic
execution (Fig. 8a–f). Across the evaluated tasks, it achieved higher success than OpenVLA2 , OpenVLA-OFT20 , UniVLA22 ,
and WorldVLA21 .
During pouring (Fig. 8c), changing loads and visual occlusion challenged stable execution. The cerebellar module integrated
proprioceptive feedback at 200 Hz, allowing motor commands to be adjusted as the physical state changed. In flask shaking
(Fig. 8e), coordinated repeated motions were consistent with the temporal context provided by the recurrent and spiking states.
These task observations complement the stabilization measurements and temporal ablation above.
We then introduced an unexpected obstruction during the “Recover to Safe Area” task (Fig. 8g). The evaluated VLA
baselines achieved 0.0% recovery and often continued executing the obstructed trajectory. NeuroVLA achieved 54.8% recovery.
Following a wrench transient, the local cerebellar–spinal pathway generated a withdrawal response with a reported latency
below 50 ms, bypassing the cortical inference loop (> 200 ms). Local trajectory correction then supported recovery and task
continuation.
Together with the other physical tasks, the collision result supports the use of bodily feedback and local motor state to
complement semantic instruction following. The limits of recovery are considered in the Discussion.

3 Discussion
Beyond architectural inertia from language modeling
The success of language-model pretraining makes its architectures and scaling strategies a natural starting point for embodied
intelligence. However, this starting point can become a constraint if motor competence is treated primarily as an extension of
semantic sequence generation. Our results motivate a broader design agenda: high-frequency proprioceptive sensing, persistent
motor state, and local corrective pathways should be explicit architectural components, rather than capabilities assumed to
emerge from semantic scaling alone. This position complements progress in vision-language reasoning and continuous-action
experts by addressing the bodily interface through which their outputs are executed.

Motor memory and efficient execution
The term “muscle memory” motivates a concrete design requirement here: a motor controller should retain execution-relevant
state and use recent sensorimotor history to shape its next action. In NeuroVLA, recurrent proprioceptive context and persistent
membrane dynamics provide this short-term motor memory. The temporal ablation supports the usefulness of retained state for
task execution; it does not establish the long-term skill consolidation associated with biological motor learning. Distinguishing
these timescales makes the analogy testable and links it to implementable control mechanisms.
The multi-step ablation supports the value of persistent motor state, while selective firing shows how neural activity can
follow actuation demand. FPGA measurements establish low latency and energy use for the spinal controller. These findings
support hardware-efficient local execution, but low spinal power does not imply that the complete VLA system or robot operates
at the same power. The hardware inference measurement is also distinct from end-to-end collision-response latency.
Limits of local recovery and future work
Local force feedback improved recovery in the tested contact scenario, yet a 54.8% success rate does not establish a general
safety guarantee. Broader perturbation tests and explicit sensing-to-actuation latency measurements would better characterize
the operating limits. Comparisons in this study concern the selected baselines and tasks, rather than all VLA architectures.
Training currently relies on offline behaviour cloning and GPU-based surrogate gradients. Although the spinal controller is
validated on an FPGA-based neuromorphic processor, the cortical and cerebellar modules remain on the CUDA tier. Online
plasticity and deployment of additional control components on neuromorphic hardware are future directions35 . Simulation

4 Methods
4.1 Architecture Overview
We formalize the embodied control task as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)36 . At each time step t,
the agent receives a multimodal observation tuple ot ∈ O, comprising high-dimensional visual sensory data It ∈ RH×W ×3 , a
natural language instruction L, and a history of low-dimensional proprioceptive states st−H:t ∈ RH×Ds , where H denotes the
size of history window and Ds represents the dimensions of joint positions, velocities, and end-effector forces (6-DoF wrench).
The objective is to generate a sequence of continuous motor actions at ∈ A to maximize the cumulative reward.
To reconcile the conflicting computational requirements of semantic planning (high-latency, abstract) and motor execution
(low-latency, precise), we propose the Tri-Level Neuromorphic Vision-Language-Action ( NeuroVLA) architecture. As
shown in Fig. 1, the system control policy π(at |ot ) is decomposed into a hierarchical composition of three specialized mapping
functions:
at = Φspine (Φcerebellum (Φcortex (It , L), ht )) ,
where Φcortex , Φcerebellum , and Φspine correspond to the cortical, cerebellar, and spinal modules, respectively. ht denotes the
compact dynamic context vector from the neurological state estimator. This decomposition enforces a strict division of labor
across timescales and hardware substrates:
1. Cortical Semantic Projection (Φcortex ): Operates on the CUDA Computing Tier. It maps the visual-linguistic inputs
(It , L) into a semantic latent intention space zsem ∈ RDmodel , where Dmodel denotes the dimension of model internal feature
space. This module utilizes a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone to extract open-vocabulary affordances, without
requiring a new semantic update at every local control step.
2. Cerebellar State Modulation (Φcerebellum ): Operates on the CUDA Computing Tier. It functions as a state-dependent
affine transformation. A recurrent estimator aggregates the proprioceptive history st−H:t into a dynamic context vector
ht . This context is then used to modulate the semantic latent zsem via a Gated Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)
mechanism, yielding a refined, physics-aware latent code zmod . This process is inspired by cerebellar feedback modulation;
it conditions the action latent on observed physical state.

4.2 Cortical Module: Semantic Latent Generation
The Cortical Module occupies the highest tier of the control hierarchy, tasked with cross-modal reasoning and the synthesis
of abstract motor intentions from high-dimensional observations. Unlike lower-level circuits that must react to instantaneous
physical dynamics, the cortical layer operates on a semantic timescale, prioritizing long-horizon planning and generalization
over high-frequency control.
4.2.1 Vision-Language Reasoning Backbone

We leverage a pre-trained Large Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Qwen-VL32 , as the general-purpose reasoning
engine. Formally, given the current RGB observation It and the natural language instruction L, the VLM functions as a dense
feature extractor:
Ht = FVLM (It , L; θvlm ),

of VLM. This rich representation captures scene geometry cues, object semantics, and linguistic context, providing a multimodal
scene representation that supports open-vocabulary reasoning and manipulation.
4.2.2 Layer-wise Semantic Distillation

Directly feeding the high-dimensional, variable-length VLM hidden states into a motor policy is computationally prohibitive
and prone to overfitting. To bridge the dimensionality gap between the VLM’s linguistic-visual space and the robot’s control
manifold, we employ a Layer-wise Querying Transformer (Q-Former).
The Q-Former acts as a learnable information bottleneck. It utilizes a set of learnable query tokens Q ∈ RK×D to attend
to specific subsets of the VLM’s hidden representations, where K denotes the number of tokens and D denotes the token
dimension. Crucially, rather than utilizing only the final layer, we extract features from a designated range of intermediate
layer index [lstart , lend ] to capture both low-level spatial details and high-level semantic abstractions. The extraction process is
formalized as:
zsem = Q-Former(Concat(Ht [lstart : lend ]), Q; θQ-Former ),
where zsem ∈ RK×Daction denotes the Semantic Latent Intention and θQ-Former denotes the parameters of Q-Former.

4.3 Cerebellar Module: Gated Recurrent Neuromodulation
While the cortical module generates semantic intentions, it operates significantly slower than the dynamics of physical
interaction and lacks access to high-frequency proprioceptive feedback. To bridge this gap, the Cerebellar Module functions as
an adaptive filter, performing dynamic state estimation and trajectory refinement before the signals reach the spinal execution
layer.
4.3.1 Proprioceptive State Estimation via Recurrent Dynamics

Inspired by the integration of bodily feedback in biological motor control, we employ a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)34 as
a recurrent state estimator. Let st−H:t ∈ RH×Ds denote the history of robot states over a horizon H (aligned with the sensor
frequency, e.g., 50 Hz), where each state vector includes joint angles, velocities, and 6-DoF wrench measurements. The
estimator integrates this temporal sequence to produce a compact dynamic context vector ht :
ht = GRU(st−H:t ; θgru ),
where θgru denotes the parameter of the designed GRU. Unlike static encoders (e.g., MLPs), the GRU captures the rate of
change and contact transients (e.g., collision impulses), which are critical for stabilizing motion under perturbation.

4.3.2 Gated Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)

The core computational role of the cerebellum is to modulate cortical commands based on current physical constraints. We
formalize this interaction using a Gated FiLM mechanism28 . First, to prevent proprioceptive noise from overwhelming semantic
intent during stable phases, we employ a learnable gating factor gt :
gt = σ (Wg · Proj(ht )),
where σ , Wg and Proj(·) denote the sigmoid function, the learnable affine transformation matrix and the projection function.
This gate selectively regulates how much physical context is allowed to influence the cortical plan. Subsequently, the dynamic
context ht is projected into affine transformation parameters—scale γ t and shift β t —which are applied to the cortical semantic
latent zsem :
γ t = fγ (ht ), β t = fβ (ht )
zmod = (1 + γ t ) ⊙ (zsem · gt ) + β t
, where ⊙ denotes the element-wise Hadamard product. This operation allows the cerebellar module to strictly enforce gain
control: for instance, upon detecting a collision (spike in ht ), the network can suppress the forward velocity encoded in zsem
(via γ t ≈ −1) and inject a retraction bias (via β t ), effectively rewriting the motor plan in real-time.

performs K cycles of internal recurrence (set to K = 2 in our experiments). In each iteration k ∈ {0, ..., K − 1}, the module
predicts a tentative action latent, updates the anticipated state evolution, and re-modulates the input. Formally:

4.4.3 Continuous Integration for Motor Decoding

A critical challenge in applying SNNs to robotics is bridging the gap between discrete, stochastic spikes and the continuous
domain of joint actuation. We resolve this by implementing a Continuous Integration Protocol at the output layer40 . Unlike
hidden interneurons which undergo rapid repolarization (reset) upon spiking, the output motor neurons are configured as
non-resetting integrators. They continuously accumulate synaptic influx over the simulation window without discharging
their potential:
at [τ] = Wout · uout [τ],
where uout represents the accumulated membrane voltage. This mechanism serves two control functions:
1. Temporal Smoothing: The integration process acts as a natural low-pass filter, converting high-frequency spike trains
into smooth, continuous motor trajectories. This mirrors the biological process of twitch summation, where muscles
integrate neural drive into continuous force, filtering out neural noise.
2. Kinematic Consistency: By maintaining the voltage state, the network naturally enforces temporal consistency in the
output action sequence, reducing kinematic jerk and ensuring fluid transitions between control steps.
